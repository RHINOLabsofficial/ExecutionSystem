University vs. Starting a Business

The founding choice of your twenties

This is the decision most people make by default. They go to university because everyone else does. A few start businesses because they're impatient. The right answer depends entirely on who you are — and what kind of leverage you're trying to build.

University

  • Credential that opens specific doors (IB, consulting, law, medicine)
  • Structured learning with low downside risk
  • Peer network at a critical developmental age
  • Time to experiment before committing
  • Access to campus recruiting pipelines

University — The Real Costs

  • Opportunity cost: 3–5 years of not building
  • Structured learning ≠ applicable skill
  • Most degrees don't teach how money works
  • Peer group is often not high-ambition
  • Delays the feedback loop that builds judgment

Building a Business Early

  • Real market feedback in months, not years
  • Forces financial literacy immediately
  • Compounding experience — 2 years of failure > 4 years of class
  • No ceiling on income during building phase
  • Optionality: you can still do a Master's later

Business — The Real Risks

  • No safety net if revenue fails
  • Certain paths permanently closed (IB analyst programs, Big Law)
  • Isolation without the right peer network
  • High failure rate in years 1–2
  • No credential to fall back on

⚠ The Biggest Mistake

Going to university while wanting to build a business because it "feels safer." Safety is an illusion when the opportunity cost is your most productive years. University delays the feedback loop that makes entrepreneurs good. Do not confuse structure with progress.

📊 Case Study — Two Paths at 22

Path A: Lucas, 22, builds a digital services business. Year 1: €18k revenue. Year 2: €55k. Year 3: €120k. At 25 he has 3 years of real operating experience, a client portfolio, and financial independence.

Path B: Max, 22, studies Business Administration. At 25 he starts an internship at €1,400/month. At 27 he earns €55k/year. At 30, €75k with bonus — stable, but 5 years behind in compounding experience.

Verdict: Path A wins on income velocity. Path B wins on institutional access. The decision depends entirely on your destination.

When University Wins

  • Your target is IB, Big Law, Medicine, or Elite Consulting
  • You have no clear business idea and need time to explore
  • You're targeting roles where university brand matters for filtering
  • Tuition is subsidized (Germany, Netherlands) — low financial risk

When Business Wins

  • You have a validated idea or strong freelance skill
  • Your target path does not require a degree (tech, media, services)
  • You are willing to invest 2 years of low income for asymmetric upside
  • You are self-directed and disciplined without external structure

Chapter 2 · Career Decisions

Finance vs. Consulting

Two elite tracks. Two completely different games.

Finance and consulting are the two most-coveted career tracks among ambitious graduates. Both are prestigious. Both pay well. But they build entirely different skill sets, networks, and exit options — and choosing the wrong one costs you years.

Finance is about capital allocation. You learn how money flows, how assets are valued, how deals are structured. Consulting is about problem-solving and communication. You learn to dissect complex organizational problems, build frameworks, and present recommendations. The real question is: what competency do you want to compound for the next decade?

Finance (IB / PE / HF)

  • IB Analyst Year 1: €70–110k base + bonus
  • PE Associate: €120–200k all-in
  • Hedge Fund: up to €500k+ with carry
  • Narrow but extremely deep skillset
  • Best exits: PE, HF, VC, CFO track

Consulting (MBB / Big4)

  • MBB Analyst Year 1: €65–90k + bonus
  • Consultant: €90–130k all-in
  • Senior Manager: €150–250k
  • Broad problem-solving skillset
  • Best exits: C-Suite, startup, PE ops
💡 The Key Insight

Finance builds financial capital and quantitative intuition. Consulting builds intellectual frameworks and executive presence. If you want to manage money, go finance. If you want to lead organizations or build companies, consulting develops more transferable skills. Most CFOs come from finance. Many CEOs and founders come from consulting.

Startup vs. Corporate

Speed vs. stability — and why the choice isn't what you think

The startup vs. corporate debate has become ideological. Startups have been glorified. Corporates have been dismissed. Both conclusions are wrong. The right environment depends on what you're trying to learn, earn, and build in the next 3–5 years.

Early-Stage Startup (Seed–Series B)

  • Extreme learning velocity — you own entire functions
  • Equity upside if the company succeeds
  • Direct access to founders and decision-makers
  • Resume signal: "I built something real"
  • High internal optionality — pivot fast

Corporate / Blue Chip

  • Stable income and structured progression
  • Brand on resume opens future doors
  • Deep domain expertise in one function
  • Resources, mentorship, training programs
  • Lower personal risk, more predictable trajectory
⚠ The Startup Romanticization Trap

90% of startups fail within 5 years. "Working at a startup" can mean anything from Series D hypergrowth to a 3-person experiment with no product-market fit. A bad startup gives you chaos, low pay, and a resume gap. A great startup (Tier 1 VC-backed, strong founding team) is often superior to any corporate role.

Chapter 4 · Career Decisions

Generalist vs. Specialist

The T-Shape question every ambitious professional must answer

In a world where AI is commoditizing broad skills at speed, the generalist vs. specialist question has never been more important. The answer is not binary — the architecture of your expertise may be the single most important career decision you make in your 20s.
💡 The T-Shape Model

The highest-value profile is neither pure generalist nor pure specialist. It is T-shaped: extreme depth in one area (the vertical bar) combined with broad fluency across adjacent disciplines (the horizontal bar). Pure generalists are replaceable. Pure specialists are fragile. T-shapes are scarce and therefore valuable.

Generalist Profile

  • Adaptable to different contexts and industries
  • Strong in leadership, coordination, strategy
  • High ceiling: CEO, founder, chief of staff
  • Risk: "Jack of all trades" perception early-career
  • Ideal for: consulting, entrepreneurship, VC

Deep Specialist (T-Shape)

  • Becomes "the person" for a specific problem
  • Harder to replace, easier to charge premium
  • High value in AI era — rare human expertise
  • Strong at freelancing, advisory, consulting
  • Ideal for: finance, engineering, law, niche SaaS
⚠ The AI Generalist Trap

AI is commoditizing generalist knowledge at unprecedented speed. Tasks that required a broad-skilled analyst — research, summarizing, structuring arguments, writing drafts — are increasingly automated. What AI cannot replicate: domain intuition built from years of practitioner experience, proprietary network access, and highly specialized judgment. The more niche your expertise, the higher your AI-era moat.

Your Specialization Design

Design your T-shape profile.

⚡

The Strategic Verdict

Age 20–25: be a generalist — explore broadly. Age 25–32: commit to your vertical. Develop extreme depth in one domain. Build the horizontal bar deliberately. The goal is to become the person who gets called when a specific high-stakes problem needs solving — not the person who "knows a bit about everything."

Is another 2 years in academia worth it?

AI Builder vs. AI User

Are you building the tools — or using them to build your advantage?

Most AI advice tells you to "learn AI." That's too vague to be useful. The real question is: are you going to build AI systems, or use AI systems to become dramatically more productive in your existing domain? Both are valid. They require completely different investments.

AI Builder

  • Builds models, agents, pipelines, products
  • Requires: Python, ML fundamentals, LLM APIs
  • Income ceiling: extremely high (ML engineer / technical founder)
  • Time to competency: 12–24 months of focused learning
  • Best for: people with technical aptitude who enjoy programming

AI Power User

  • Uses AI to 5–20x output in existing domain
  • Requires: prompt engineering, workflow design, tool fluency
  • Effect: dramatically increases value per hour
  • Time to competency: 30–90 days of focused practice
  • Best for: anyone who wants leverage now, without coding
💡 The 2025 AI Leverage Reality

The highest ROI in 2025 is not learning to build AI. It is learning to use AI better than 99% of people in your specific domain. A consultant who uses Claude to do in 2 hours what took 2 days is not "an AI consultant" — they are simply a dramatically more productive consultant. Domain expertise + AI fluency = disproportionate output. That is the moat.

Chapter 3 · AI Decisions

AI Freelancer vs. AI Founder

Two ways to monetize AI skills — with very different risk profiles

If you have AI skills, you face a choice: sell your skills as a service (freelancer) or build a product that scales without your direct labor (founder). Both work. The decision is about risk tolerance, capital, and long-term vision.

AI Freelancer

  • Revenue in 30–60 days if you have skills
  • Low risk — no product to build
  • High income ceiling: €150–500/hr for specialized work
  • Income = direct function of hours worked
  • Best for: early stage, skill validation, capital building

AI Founder

  • Revenue in 6–18 months if you succeed
  • High risk — product, market, distribution all uncertain
  • Income ceiling: unlimited (equity, ARR, exit)
  • Income = independent of hours when it works
  • Best for: validated problem + existing distribution
💡 The Sequencing Strategy

The optimal path for most people: start as an AI freelancer. Build income, credibility, and — most importantly — a deep understanding of what problems clients actually have. Then, after 12–18 months, identify the most recurring pain point and productize the solution. The transition from freelancer to founder is much smoother when funded by existing revenue.

Chapter 2 · Business Decisions

Agency vs. Digital Products

Time-for-money vs. build-once, sell-always

An agency sells your time. A digital product sells your knowledge or software — once, and then again, and again. These two models create completely different businesses, lifestyles, and wealth trajectories. Understanding the difference may be the most important business decision you make in your 20s.

Agency Model

  • Revenue: fast (1–8 weeks from zero)
  • Margin: 30–60% after team costs
  • Scale: requires more people to grow
  • Client dependency: high
  • Personal leverage: low
  • Exit value: low (people business)

Digital Products

  • Revenue: slower (3–12 months typically)
  • Margin: 70–95% (near-zero COGS)
  • Scale: unlimited without team growth
  • Client dependency: none (audience-dependent)
  • Personal leverage: very high
  • Exit value: high (recurring revenue multiple)
💡 The Hybrid Path

The smartest founders use agency revenue to fund product development. Run the agency at 50–60% of your capacity. Use the remaining time and cash flow to build the product. When product revenue exceeds your salary needs, wind down the agency. This is not theory — it is the actual path most successful bootstrapped founders took.

The Strategic Verdict

If you need income now: start an agency or consulting practice. If you have 6–12 months of runway: go directly to digital products. If you want the safest path to product income: run both simultaneously. The goal is to escape time-for-money as quickly as your financial situation allows.

Chapter 3 · Business Decisions

Creator Business vs. Consulting

Public audience vs. private expertise — two paths, same destination

The creator economy and the consulting world are converging. The best consultants build audiences. The best creators sell expertise. But starting in each world requires different initial moves, builds different assets, and attracts different clients.

Creator Business

  • Asset: audience and distribution
  • Income: slow to start, scales with audience
  • Leverage: very high (platform distribution)
  • Personal brand required: yes
  • Risk: algorithm and platform dependency
  • Best for: educators, opinionated experts, storytellers

Consulting

  • Asset: expertise and client relationships
  • Income: fast to start, plateaus without leverage
  • Leverage: low (without productizing)
  • Personal brand: optional but helps
  • Risk: client concentration
  • Best for: deep specialists, operators, advisors
💡 The Convergence Strategy

The most powerful modern business: Consulting → builds expertise and case studies → Creator business → builds audience that generates consulting leads → Digital products → monetizes audience at scale. Each phase funds the next. This is the structure behind every "overnight success" in the expert economy.

Chapter 1 · Investing Decisions

The Wealth Architecture

Building wealth is not about picking stocks — it is about building a system

Wealth is not built by finding the best investment. It is built by designing a system that automatically converts income into assets over time. Before you invest a single euro, you need to understand the structure of your wealth architecture.
💡 The Four-Layer Wealth Stack

Layer 1 — Emergency Fund: 3–6 months of expenses in cash. Non-negotiable before investing anything else. Layer 2 — Tax-Advantaged Accounts: Use available tax vehicles (bAV in Germany) first. Never leave employer matching unclaimed. Layer 3 — Index Core: 70–80% of investable capital in broad-market ETFs. The engine of long-term wealth. Layer 4 — Satellite / Growth: 20–30% in higher-risk assets (individual stocks, crypto, angel).

⚠ The #1 Wealth Mistake in Your 20s

Waiting until you have "enough" money to invest. A €200/month investment at 8% annual return started at age 22 is worth more at 65 than a €500/month investment started at 35. Compound interest punishes delay more severely than it rewards late increases. Start immediately, regardless of amount.

Your Network Architecture

Most people's networks are accidents. Yours doesn't have to be.

The average person builds their network through proximity — they befriend colleagues, classmates, and neighbors. This is the least effective way to build a network for ambitious goals. Strategic networking is not about collecting contacts. It is about deliberately cultivating relationships with people who are either where you want to be, or on the same trajectory.
💡 The Network Proximity Effect

Your income tends toward the average of the 5 people you spend the most time with. This is not mysticism — it is the result of shared standards, mutual introductions, information flow, and psychological benchmarking. If everyone around you earns €50k and considers it success, your ambition calibration adjusts downward. Proximity to high performers upgrades your defaults.

Chapter 2 · Network Decisions

Mentors & Strategic Advisors

The fastest way to compress a decade of learning into 2 years

The single highest-ROI network investment for anyone under 30 is finding mentors who have already solved the problems you are facing. A mentor does not give you the answer — they give you the pattern recognition that took them 10 years to build, compressed into a conversation.
💡 What Mentors Actually Give You

Most people seek mentors for advice. But the real value is different: Introductions (a warm introduction from a trusted person is worth 50 cold outreaches). Calibration (mentors who have done extraordinary things reframe your ceiling of what's possible). Early warnings (mistakes obvious in hindsight but invisible in the moment).

How to Get a Mentor Who Doesn't Have Time for You

1

Give before you ask

Share relevant research, make an introduction, write a thoughtful comment on their work — three times before making any request. The first ask from a stranger gets ignored. The first ask from someone who has already contributed gets considered.

2

Ask for 20 minutes, not mentorship

"Would you be open to a 20-minute call to share how you made the transition from consulting to PE?" is 10x more likely to get a yes than "Could you mentor me?" Be specific. Be brief. Make it easy to say yes.

3

Deliver value before asking again

After the first meeting, send a thank-you with specific takeaways. A week later, share something relevant to their work. Make every interaction valuable to them. Mentorship is earned through consistent demonstrated seriousness.

4

Execute visibly and close the loop

The fastest way to convert a one-time meeting into an ongoing relationship: do what they recommended and report back with results. People want to see their advice work. Closing the loop is the rarest thing in the world and the most powerful signal of seriousness.

Chapter 3 · Network Decisions

Personal Positioning & Communities

Being found instead of seeking — the highest-leverage networking strategy

The highest-leverage networking strategy is not going to events — it is creating the conditions that bring the right people to you. A well-positioned LinkedIn profile, a clear public point of view, or a niche newsletter can generate better inbound relationships than years of attending industry events.

Offline Communities

  • Alumni networks (university, company)
  • Industry conferences and events
  • Local business or investor groups
  • Mastermind groups (peer accountability)
  • Professional associations (CFA, CAIA, etc.)

High-Quality Online Communities

  • Paid communities with high entry standards
  • Founder communities (YC alumni, Indie Hackers)
  • Finance & investment circles (niche Discord/Slack)
  • Cohort-based courses with active peer networks
  • LinkedIn and X/Twitter professional communities
💡 The Positioning Formula

[Specific Expertise] + [Specific Audience] + [Clear Point of View] = Magnetic Positioning.

Example: "I help pre-Series A founders build investor-ready financial models and narratives." — Not: "I'm interested in finance and startups." The specificity is what makes you memorable, referable, and findable.
